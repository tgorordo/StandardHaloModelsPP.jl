Performance Tips

Evaluating a Distribution at Many Points

Each call like fχ(m, v) first computes the model's normalizations, which for a model carrying Measurements costs several times the evaluation itself. To evaluate repeatedly, freeze the model once, and call the result:

f = fχ(SHMpp)           # normalizations computed once
f.(vs)                  # or f(v) in a loop, a closure, your own integrand, ...

flab = fχlab(SHMpp, t)  # also fixes the time, and so the Earth's velocity
flab(v)

The same works for fR, fS, fRlab and fχlab (fχlab(m) takes ([t,] v); fχlab(m, t) takes v).

Broadcasting over velocities with a single model does this for you: fχ.(m, vs), fχlab.(m, t, vs) and the default-model fχ.(vs) all freeze the model once. For 1000 points of SHMpp this is about 7x faster than calling fχ(m, v) per point. Broadcasting over an array of models still evaluates each model separately, as usual.

Uncertainties Cost Time

A model carrying Measurements (such as SHMpp) makes every evaluation propagate uncertainties: about 10x slower pointwise. When you don't need them, use its central-value model value(m) instead, which evaluates as fast as an exact one.

Integrals evaluate central values by default; uncertainty = true propagates the model's uncertainties, at roughly 10-130x the cost (most for g and h).

Analytic Fast Paths

For a model without the anisotropic component ($\eta = 0$, i.e. every preset but SHMpp), with the Earth inside its escape sphere, fχspeed, g and h use exact closed forms [16, Sec. I.4], which take under 100 ns. Pass analytic = false to force the numerical integrals instead (e.g. to check them).

Choosing Between velint and speedint

For a kernel that depends only on the speed $|\mathbf{v}|$, both velint (the 3-D velocity integral) and speedint (a 1-D integral over the speed distribution) apply:

  • For $\eta = 0$ models, speedint uses the analytic speed distribution and is the faster: around 20 µs, against 0.04-0.2 ms for velint.
  • For anisotropic models (e.g. SHMpp), every point of the speed distribution is itself an angular integral, and velint is faster (about 0.04-2 ms against 0.4-5 ms, both at a relative tolerance of $10^{-4}$). Both get cheaper as $v_\text{min}$ rises.

speedint defaults to a tighter reltol ($10^{-6}$) than velint ($10^{-4}$). For an anisotropic model comparing defaults is a misleading benchmark (a speedint takes around 50 ms). Pass reltol explicitly when comparing the two, or when the default precision isn't needed.

A kernel depending on the direction of $\mathbf{v}$ needs velint.

Tolerances

Every integrator defaults to a purely relative tolerance: a reltol suited to its method, and abstol = 0. To set an absolute tolerance, give abstol as a Quantity in the units of the result. A plain number is taken in the integrand's internal units, so it only makes sense for integrals of order one: a small integral (e.g. a rate for a small cross-section, or $g$ far into its tail) with a plain-number abstol can stop well short of its reltol.

Many Integrals: Sweeps and Threads

To compute an integral at many times, $v_\text{min}$ values or kernel parameters, solve an EnsembleProblem of HaloIntegralProblems on threads, as described under Sweeps. Start Julia with threads (e.g. julia -t auto) for EnsembleThreads() to run in parallel.

To integrate against the distribution inside your own integrals, use the forms frozen at a time, fχspeed(m, t) and fχlab(m, t), rather than calling fχspeed(m, t, v) or fχlab(m, t, v) at every point (see Building Your Own Integrals). Likewise, to evaluate g or h at many $v_\text{min}$ at one time (e.g. across a recoil-energy spectrum), use g(m, t) and h(m, t): on the analytic path they take around 25-40 ns per call, against 100-130 ns for g(m, t, vmin).
